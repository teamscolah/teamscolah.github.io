Amina is born at home, in a village two hours from the nearest clinic. Her birth is registered six weeks later, when her mother brings her for her first vaccines. She gets her first two doses of the pentavalent vaccine on time. Then the family moves to another district to farm, and nobody notices that her third dose never happens. A year later she appears in two clinic registers under two spellings, and in neither as fully vaccinated.

Every part of that story is common, and every part is a data problem. This post follows one child through the immunisation life cycle, shows where children go missing, and explains how Scolah Compass keeps track of them at each step. It ends with how an institution can come to own the system that does it.

## Where children go missing

- **Births that are never registered.** A child who isn't on any list can't be called for a vaccine.
- **Doses that are never recorded.** Outreach sessions happen where there is no network, and a paper tally sheet is copied, late or never, into a register.
- **Defaulters nobody follows up.** A missed third dose looks the same as a child who moved, died or was vaccinated somewhere else.
- **Stock that runs out or expires.** A session without enough doses sends families home. Doses that expire in a store are paid for twice.
- **Counts that don't match.** The same child in two registers makes coverage look better than it is, while the child who moved is counted nowhere.

### 1. Register the birth

Amina's record can start in two ways. When civil registration already has her birth, [Scolah Compass Exchange](https://docs.scolah.com/en/exchange/) reads it from the registration system, for example OpenCRVS, and creates her record. When a health worker meets a child whose birth was never registered, Exchange can send a birth notification to the civil registry, so a registrar can complete it. Either way, Amina is linked to her household and its area from the start.

### 2. Enrol in the programme

Amina is enrolled in the immunisation programme like any other Scolah Compass programme. Her record shows which programmes she's in, and a timeline records every step: when she was registered, enrolled and vaccinated, and by whom.

### 3. Plan each dose

The national schedule lives in the disbursement engine, the same engine that runs cash and in-kind payments. Each dose, such as BCG, the three pentavalent doses or measles, is set up once as a **cycle** for an in-kind item: the vaccine. When a session is planned, a batch is created from the cycle and its filters select the children due, by programme, area and date of birth. Caregivers can be reminded by SMS, WhatsApp or USSD, using the same filters.

### 4. Vaccinate, and supply the doses

Each dose given is recorded against the child, with proof that it was given, such as a photo of the signed child health card. Staff at a clinic record doses and proof in the Scolah Compass web portal. Vaccinators at outreach sessions use the Scolah Compass mobile app, which works offline and syncs when the network returns.

The doses themselves are handled by [Scolah Compass Resources](https://docs.scolah.com/en/resources/). The vaccine in a dose cycle is linked to a stock item in Resources. A batch reserves the doses its session needs, and each dose given is recorded as a stock movement. Resources also tracks batches and expiry dates when vaccines are received, transfers between stores, and stock counts, so a district can see what it holds and what will expire first.

### 5. Follow up missed doses

After a session, the batch shows which children received their dose and which are still pending. Those still pending are the defaulters. Caseworkers can follow them up with reminders, cases and home visits, each recorded on the child's timeline. When Amina's family moves, her record moves with her: a health worker in the new district sees the doses she has had and the one she is missing.

<Figure
	src={followUpCase}

When the same child is registered twice, for example under two spellings in two clinics, duplicate review flags the pair. Matching is done by [Kinlink](https://github.com/teamscolah/kinlink), our open-source record-matching engine, which compares names, dates of birth and phone numbers even when they were written differently.

### 6. Report

Indicators track coverage, doses given and children enrolled, by area and month, against the targets the programme sets. Exchange sends the figures to DHIS2, so the national health information system gets them without anyone typing them in again.

### 7. Complete

When Amina has had her last scheduled dose, graduation rules put her forward for review, and her immunisation enrolment is closed. Her record and its timeline stay, ready for the next programme she joins, such as school health or a child grant.

## Owning the system, not just using it

National immunisation programmes run for decades. The system behind them should belong to the country, not depend on one supplier for every change. Through Enterprise Technology Transfer, an institution can come to own Scolah Compass for its programme.

**It runs on the institution's own servers**, in country if required, with the source code for Scolah Compass, Scolah Compass Resources and Scolah Compass Exchange, and the build instructions, database migrations, API specifications and tests that go with them.

**For an agreed period, we build it together.** The institution's own engineers join our engineering team. They take part in planning, design, building, code review, testing and releases, with a named Scolah technical lead, and our engineers spend time on site with them every year. They can change the system with us or without us. Changes to the shared code go through the same review, tests and security checks as ours, so the code stays stable and the institution is never left on its own.

**After that period, the code is theirs.** The institution takes full ownership of the code for its programme. It can't resell it, but it can keep running, changing and releasing it with its own team, who by then have been doing exactly that for years.
